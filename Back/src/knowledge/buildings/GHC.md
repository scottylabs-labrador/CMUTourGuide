# Gates and Hillman Centers

## Overview

Gates and Hillman Centers for Future-Generation Technologies are home to the School of Computer Science and Computational Biology Department. These Gold LEED Certified Centers were made possible by lead gifts from the Bill and Melinda Gates and the Henry L. Hillman foundations. The building also houses a Mission Control Center for the student project Iris Rover created by over 300 students from across the university that was the first of its kind to survive a launch and a zero-gravity environment.

The School of Computer Science was the first standalone school of Computer Science in the nation and launched the first Artificial Intelligence degree by a US University. The school houses five undergraduate majors including Computer Science, Artificial Intelligence, Computational Biology, Human Computer Interaction, Robotics.

Completed in 2009 and dedicated September 22, 2009, GHC is the home of the School of Computer Science at 4902 Forbes Avenue. It's 217,000 sq ft across nine stories, designed by Mack Scogin Merrill Elam Architects, LEED Gold certified, built with a $20 million gift from the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation and $10 million from the Hillman Foundation. A cistern captures 500,000 gallons of rain and snowmelt per year for toilet flushing, and 98% of construction waste (19,000 tons) was diverted from landfill. The building won a 2012 AIA Institute Honor Award for Architecture, one of just nine worldwide that year. The complex navigates an unusually steep 75-foot site-elevation change, and the signature feature is The Helix — a long spiral ramp inside the Gates atrium that traverses several stories, famous for unofficial student stunts including mountain-bike descents and office-chair races. The black zinc diamond-shingle cladding is a modern reinterpretation of Hornbostel's brick; each of 310+ windows has its own silver zinc surround. The Randy Pausch Memorial Bridge to Purnell features 7,000+ programmable LED lights and symbolically links computer science to drama — Pausch taught both. Its opening lighting sequences depicted six visual metaphors from his book "The Last Lecture" (Fun with Crayons, Outer Space, Make the Most of Each Day, Be the First Penguin, The Elevator in Randy's Room, Disney and the Circus), and abstract penguin cut-outs in the bridge's structure honor his "first penguin" message. Rashid Auditorium seats 250; its seats carry a cryptic binary pattern long rumored by students to be ASCII (analysts confirmed it's not). Other named spaces include the Luis von Ahn "Awesome Classroom" (gift of the Duolingo and reCAPTCHA founder, CS '03/'05), the Raj Reddy Conference Room, and the Moonshot Mission Control for lunar rover missions. Dining is Rohr Café on floor 3 — espresso, sandwiches, and a popular study spot with one of the healthier menus on campus. The Iris Rover launched January 8, 2024 aboard Astrobotic's Peregrine lander on the maiden flight of the Vulcan Centaur rocket. Built by 300+ students at just 4 pounds and shoebox-sized, Iris was billed as the first American, first university-developed, smallest, and lightest robotic lunar rover. Important accuracy note: the Peregrine lander suffered a propellant leak hours after launch and never reached the Moon, burning up over the Pacific on January 18, 2024 — but Iris successfully powered on and communicated from space, and a replica is displayed at the Smithsonian. Turing Award winners Raj Reddy, Manuel Blum, and Edmund M. Clarke are associated with the building, alongside pioneers Allen Newell and Herbert Simon, Randy Pausch ("The Last Lecture"), and former faculty member Geoffrey Hinton (2024 Nobel in Physics).
