# Scaife Hall

## Overview

Alan Magee Scaife Hall of Engineering is a new 85,000 square-foot building comprising labs, a drone arena, offices, classrooms, and a café. The labs support research in a variety of subjects ranging from Biology to Robotics to Rapid Prototyping and are supported by 100 PhD workspaces directly adjacent to the lab spaces. The building is the home for the Mechanical Engineering Department.

Grand opening was November 9–10, 2023 for the new 85,000-sq-ft Scaife — double the size of the original 1962 building (the famous "Potato Chip" auditorium, demolished 2020). Named for benefactor Alan Magee Scaife. Designed by Philadelphia firm KieranTimberlake, with lead funding from the Allegheny Foundation. The building is the home of the Department of Mechanical Engineering and uses 25% less energy than the ASHRAE 90.1-2010 baseline, with 43% offset by CMU's off-site wind farm and a net pEUI of 77 kBtu/sf. The adjacent plaza was dedicated at opening as the Gordon E. Moore Engineering Quad, honoring the Intel co-founder.

The signature feature is a double-height high-bay drone/UAV arena whose floor is patterned specifically to aid motion-capture data capture — viewable from the circulation space above. Inside: 11 research labs across levels A and B (wet labs, dry labs, robotics, biosafety level 1, rapid prototyping, tissue culturing), about 100 PhD workspaces, 80+ offices, 19 meeting rooms, and a green rooftop garden connecting to ANSYS Hall's TechSpark via large operable doors. Public art installation "Making Way" by Jessica Stockholder was commissioned to match the building's "breaking boundaries" theme. Views from the Engineering Quad extend to Junction Hollow, Schenley Park, and Pitt's Cathedral of Learning. Dining is Redhawk Coffee on the first floor, opened fall 2023 with a September 28 grand opening. Redhawk is a Pittsburgh family-owned roastery; the CMU menu includes Driftwood Oven pastries, matcha lattes, mochas with Brunton's chocolate milk, and a signature honey-cinnamon latte. Fun fact: the original 1962 Scaife's state-of-the-art computer occupied the entire fourth floor, had to be carried in by helicopter, and had less processing power than a modern smartphone.

Standing as a grand "cornerstone" of Carnegie Mellon's campus, the new Alan Magee Scaife Hall of Engineering is an 85,000-square-foot marvel completed in 2023. Replacing the original 1962 structure, this six-story facility serves as a primary entrance to the university at Frew Street, offering stunning views of Junction Hollow and Schenley Park. Architecture students will appreciate its unique three-part design: a two-story "podium" built directly into the hillside, a four-story south mass, and a two-story north mass that features a specialized portal designed specifically to allow emergency vehicles to pass through to Hamerschlag Drive.

Innovation in Action: For those interested in the future of technology, Scaife Hall is the powerhouse of the Mechanical Engineering Department. It isn't just a place for lectures; it houses a high-bay drone arena and 11 specialized labs ranging from robotics to rapid prototyping and biology. The building is designed for "intellectual vitality," featuring 19 meeting rooms, a 200-seat lecture hall, and over 100 PhD workspaces positioned directly adjacent to the labs. When students aren't busy with research, they can be found collaborating in the common areas or grabbing a coffee at the building's on-site café.

A Model of Sustainability: CMU has a deep commitment to the environment, and Scaife Hall is on track for LEED Gold certification. The building is a living classroom for sustainability; it uses 24% less energy than similar buildings and has reduced its indoor water consumption by a staggering 36% — saving over 170,000 gallons of water annually. Even the landscaping is smart, featuring a green roof and bio-retention areas that manage stormwater not just for Scaife, but also for the neighboring Baker and Porter Halls. To keep the air fresh, the building utilizes CO2 sensors that automatically cycle in outdoor air whenever it's needed.

Community and Circularity: The story of this building actually began with what was taken away — before the old Scaife Hall was demolished, CMU donated 11 tons of materials like doors and furniture to local nonprofits. During construction, the team diverted 75% of waste — about 2,500 tons — from landfills. Today, the facility encourages a "green" lifestyle by providing extensive bicycle facilities and easy access to public transit. Inside, you'll find zero-mercury LED lighting and building materials that are "red-list free," meaning they don't contain chemicals harmful to humans, ensuring a healthy environment for the next generation of engineers.
